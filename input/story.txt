Plus sixty percent Grey Market Premium. Guaranteed listing gains.

Just imagine two retail investors, Vikram and Ananya, looking at the exact same mainboard IPO priced at two hundred rupees per share. Two days before the issue closes, every financial website and WhatsApp group flashes the exact same headline: Grey Market Premium is one hundred and twenty rupees, signaling a massive sixty percent listing pop to three hundred and twenty rupees. Vikram sees that number, skips reading the company's financials, and applies with every rupee in his bank account. Ananya looks at the exact same one hundred and twenty rupee GMP, spots a hidden trap, and walks away without bidding a single rupee.

Five days later, the stock finally lists on the exchange. Instead of opening at three hundred and twenty rupees, it crashes below its issue price and opens at one hundred and eighty-five rupees, trapping Vikram in an instant loss. How did a sixty percent Grey Market Premium vanish into thin air—and how can a tiny group of dealers manufacture fake GMP hype before an IPO even closes? Let's pull back the curtain on India's unofficial grey market, because understanding the three hidden mechanics behind GMP will stop you from ever falling for manufactured listing hype again.

First, let's solve where that GMP number actually comes from. Many beginners assume Grey Market Premium is an official figure tracked by SEBI, the NSE, or the BSE. In reality, there is no exchange, no electronic order book, and zero regulatory oversight. The grey market is an informal, cash-and-trust network operating over phone calls and private messaging groups where participants trade forward commitments before shares officially list.

The math people quote is simple: Implied Listing Price equals Official Issue Price plus GMP. In Vikram's IPO, a two hundred rupee issue price plus a one hundred and twenty rupee GMP pointed to an implied price of three hundred and twenty rupees. Because no actual shares exist in demat accounts yet, traders make informal bets—and if a counterparty defaults, you have zero legal protection from SEBI or the exchanges.

To understand how hype gets engineered in this shadow market, you need to know the two secret deals dealers use to trade entire IPO applications before allotment even happens. The first is called the Kostak rate. Here, a grey market buyer pays a retail applicant a fixed fee—say, two thousand rupees—just for the rights to their IPO application, whether that application wins shares in the lottery or not. The second is called Subject to Sauda, where the buyer agrees to pay a higher fixed premium, but only if the seller actually wins allotment in the lottery; if no shares are allotted, the deal is cancelled.

Now watch how those informal deals can manufacture an illusion that traps retail investors like Vikram. Because the grey market is completely opaque and lacks audited volume data, it takes very little money for a small network of dealers to quote an artificially high GMP on Day one or Day two—especially in smaller or low-float IPOs. Financial portals scrape those quotes and publish plus sixty percent GMP across the internet.

That headline triggers massive retail fear of missing out. Everyday investors rush to submit applications before Day three closes, assuming the sixty percent profit is locked in. Yet the moment the bidding window shuts and retail capital is frozen, those thin grey market quotes dry up. Worse, on listing morning, the exchange's forty-five-minute pre-open call auction ignores the grey market completely and discovers the real opening price strictly from live institutional and public orders. If broader market sentiment turns weak or big institutions stayed away on Day three, that plus sixty percent GMP collapses overnight.

So how should you use GMP without getting burned, just like Ananya? Run this three-step reality check before you ever apply. First, never trust a single-day GMP snapshot—track whether the premium holds steady over multiple days rather than spiking right before bidding opens. Second, always verify GMP against Day three afternoon QIB subscription; real institutional money buying into the official book is ten times more reliable than an anonymous grey market quote. Third, never borrow money or use short-term leverage based on GMP, because an unofficial premium can evaporate before ten AM on listing day.
